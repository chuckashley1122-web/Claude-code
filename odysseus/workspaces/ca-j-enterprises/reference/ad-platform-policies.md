## 1. The rule that catches the most local-service copy

**Meta's personal attributes policy.** Ads must not assert or imply knowledge of
a personal attribute — including race, ethnicity, religion, beliefs, age, sexual
orientation, gender identity, disability, physical or mental health, financial
status, trade union membership, criminal history, or name.

The violation is almost always **second person**. The same claim is usually fine
in third person or stated about the business.

| Rejected | Compliant rewrite |
|---|---|
| "Struggling with your credit?" | "Financing options are available." |
| "Is your AC old and failing?" | "Most systems need replacing after 10–15 years." |
| "Homeowners over 60 — you qualify..." | "A maintenance plan for older systems." |
| "Tired of being ripped off by contractors?" | "Flat-rate pricing, quoted before we start." |
| "Your roof is damaged." | "Storm damage often shows up as..." |

The test: does the ad tell the reader something about *themselves*? If yes,
rewrite it as a statement about the world or about the business.

This is the single most common rejection reason in home-services advertising and
it is worth checking every hook against before anything else.

## 2. Special Ad Categories (Meta)

Advertisers running ads about **credit, employment, or housing** must declare a
Special Ad Category. Declaring one restricts targeting — age, gender, and ZIP
code targeting are removed or limited, and detailed targeting options are
reduced.

**Why this matters here.** These restrictions exist to prevent the digital
equivalent of redlining, and the underlying law is the same anti-discrimination
regime described in the CA-J Consulting compliance guardrails.

- **Credit** — applies to any ad promoting credit or a credit-related product.
  If CA-J Enterprises ever advertises on behalf of a lending client, or promotes
  contractor financing, this category applies.
- **Housing** — applies to real estate and housing-related opportunities. A pure
  home-repair ad is not usually a housing ad, but real-estate-adjacent
  offers can be. When unsure, declare it.
- **Employment** — applies to hiring ads, including "now hiring technicians."

**Never** attempt to reproduce restricted targeting by proxy — ZIP-adjacent
interests, income proxies, neighborhood language. That is the behaviour the
restriction exists to stop, and doing it deliberately is worse than doing it by
accident.

Google applies comparable restrictions to personalized advertising for credit,
housing, and employment. Verify the current form of both.

## 3. Claims and substantiation

Applies across Meta, Google, and the FTC Act.

**Substantiate before publishing.** Any objective claim needs support the client
can produce on request:

- "#1 in Austin", "best rated", "voted best" — needs a named, current, verifiable source
- "Licensed and insured" — needs a live license and current policy (see §5)
- "24/7 service" — needs actual round-the-clock availability
- "Same-day service" — needs it to be genuinely typical, not occasional
- "500+ five-star reviews" — needs to match the live count today
- "Family owned since 1985" — needs to be true

**Never**

- Guarantees of outcome, savings, or results the business cannot control
- "Free" where any cost, purchase, or condition attaches — the condition must be
  disclosed clearly and near the word
- Fabricated urgency: countdowns that reset, "only 3 slots left" that never changes
- Claims about a competitor that cannot be substantiated
- Fake or undisclosed testimonials — see `review-platform-policies.md`

**Handle carefully**

- "Up to X% off" — needs a real, available maximum and clear terms
- "Starting at $X" — needs jobs genuinely available at that price
- Price comparisons — need a real, current basis
- Insurance and warranty language — see §4, which is the highest-risk area here

## 4. Storm damage and insurance work — Texas-specific

**This is the highest-risk marketing area for Austin and Round Rock roofing and
restoration clients, and the rules are not obvious.** Two Texas provisions catch
storm-damage advertising specifically. Both should be confirmed with counsel
before any storm campaign runs.

**Deductibles.** Texas law restricts a contractor from advertising or promising
to pay, waive, absorb, rebate, or otherwise offset an insured's deductible on a
property insurance claim. Copy along the lines of "we'll cover your deductible",
"no out-of-pocket cost", or "deductible assistance" should be treated as
prohibited. Texas law also requires roof repair contracts paid from insurance
proceeds to carry a specific written notice regarding the deductible.

**Public adjusting.** Texas restricts a contractor from acting as a public
insurance adjuster for a property the contractor is contracted to repair, and
public adjusters are separately licensed. Copy offering to "handle your claim",
"negotiate with your insurer", or "fight the adjuster for you" risks crossing
that line.

**Safer framing for storm campaigns**

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| "We pay your deductible" | "We'll walk you through what your policy covers" |
| "No cost to you" | "Free inspection" — if the inspection genuinely is free |
| "We handle your insurance claim" | "We document damage thoroughly for your claim" |
| "We negotiate with your adjuster" | "We can meet your adjuster on site" |
| "Insurance approved" / "insurance certified" | Name the actual manufacturer certification held |

Also avoid implying any affiliation with, or endorsement by, an insurer or a
government agency — including FEMA. That implication is independently
problematic on both platforms.

## 5. Licensing claims in Texas

"Licensed and insured" is a substantiated claim, and in Texas which trades are
actually licensed is frequently misunderstood.

- **HVAC** — air conditioning and refrigeration contractors are licensed by the
  Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation.
- **Electrical** — licensed by TDLR.
- **Plumbing** — licensed by the Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners.
- **Roofing** — **Texas does not license roofing contractors at the state
  level.** A roofer may hold voluntary credentials or manufacturer
  certifications, but "licensed roofer" implies a state license that does not
  exist and should not be used. Name the actual credential instead.
- **General contracting** — no statewide general contractor license; local
  registration or permitting requirements may still apply.

Never state or imply a license the client does not hold, and never imply
licensure exists for a trade where it does not. Verify the specific client's
license number and status before any ad claims it. See
`local-market-sourcing.md` for where to check.

## 6. Google Ads specifics

**Editorial standards.** Ad text must be clear and professional. Gimmicky
capitalization ("BEST DEAL"), excessive punctuation ("Call now!!!"), repeated
words for emphasis, and non-standard spacing are rejected.

**Misrepresentation.** Covers unclear relevance, unreliable claims, misleading
ad design, and omitting material information a user needs.

**Trademarks.** Restrictions apply to using another company's trademark in ad
text. Bidding on a competitor's brand as a keyword is treated differently from
naming them in the copy — do not name a competitor's mark in ad text without
checking.

**Landing pages** must:
- work, and load reasonably on mobile
- match what the ad promised — no bait and switch
- not auto-download anything
- carry clear contact information and a privacy policy where data is collected

**Local Services Ads and the Google Guarantee** carry their own license,
insurance, and background-check requirements. Never claim "Google Guaranteed"
status the client has not actually been granted.

## 7. Pre-flight checklist

Run before any ad draft goes to a human.

**Personal attributes**
- [ ] No second-person assertion about the reader's condition, finances, health, age, or situation
- [ ] Reframed as a statement about the world or about the business

**Category**
- [ ] Credit, employment, or housing relevance assessed; Special Ad Category declared if so
- [ ] No proxy targeting substituting for restricted targeting

**Claims**
- [ ] Every objective claim has a named source the client can produce
- [ ] Superlatives sourced and current
- [ ] "Free" carries any conditions, stated clearly
- [ ] No guarantee of an outcome outside the client's control
- [ ] Urgency is real

**Texas / insurance**
- [ ] No offer to pay, waive, absorb, or rebate a deductible
- [ ] No claim to handle, negotiate, or adjust an insurance claim
- [ ] No implied affiliation with an insurer, FEMA, or any agency

**Licensing**
- [ ] License claims match the client's actual, current licenses
- [ ] No "licensed" claim for a trade Texas does not license
- [ ] License number verified where stated

**Editorial and landing page**
- [ ] No gimmicky caps, punctuation, or repetition
- [ ] No competitor trademark in ad text
- [ ] Landing page works, matches the ad, and has contact info

**Always**
- [ ] Marked DRAFT with the campaign named
- [ ] Nothing published, scheduled, or uploaded by the agent

## Verify before approval

1. **Every platform rule against current published policy** — Meta Advertising
   Standards (personal attributes, Special Ad Categories) and Google Ads
   policies (misrepresentation, editorial, trademarks, personalized advertising
   restrictions). These change; a stale rule here silently propagates.
2. **The Texas insurance provisions in §4 with counsel** — the deductible
   restriction and the contractor-as-public-adjuster restriction, including the
   required contract notice. §4 states them in general terms deliberately;
   confirm the exact statutory text, scope, and current form before any storm
   campaign runs. This is the highest-consequence section in the file.
3. **The licensing table in §5**, particularly that roofing remains unlicensed
   at state level in Texas, and which body currently licenses each trade.
4. Whether CA-J Enterprises or its clients hold Local Services Ads or Google
   Guarantee status, and what that permits saying.
5. Whether any client operates outside Texas, which would make §4 and §5
   incomplete rather than merely provisional.
