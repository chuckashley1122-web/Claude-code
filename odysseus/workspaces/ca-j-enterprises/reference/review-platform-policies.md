## 1. The FTC rule on reviews and testimonials

The FTC has a rule addressing fake and deceptive consumer reviews. It carries
civil penalty exposure, which makes this materially different from a platform
policy — a platform removes your listing; the FTC fines you.

Prohibited, in substance:

- **Fake reviews.** Reviews by someone who does not exist, who never used the
  product, or who misrepresents their experience. This includes
  AI-generated reviews presented as genuine.
- **Buying reviews** conditioned on sentiment. Paying for positive reviews, or
  paying to have negative reviews posted about a competitor.
- **Insider reviews without disclosure.** Reviews by employees, owners, family,
  or anyone with a material connection to the business, without clearly
  disclosing that connection.
- **Company-controlled review sites** presented as independent.
- **Review suppression** — using unfounded legal threats, intimidation, or false
  accusations to get a genuine negative review removed.
- **Fake social media indicators** — buying or selling followers, likes, or views
  that are bot-generated or fake, in a commercial context.

**Practical consequences for CA-J Enterprises work**

- Never draft a review. Not a sample, not a template for a customer to sign, not
  a "here's what you could say" starter. Drafting the words is manufacturing the
  review.
- Never draft a review from an employee, owner, or relative without an explicit
  disclosure of the relationship in the review itself.
- Never advise a client to threaten a reviewer. Route disputes to the platform's
  own flagging process, or to counsel.
- Testimonials used in marketing must be genuine, and material connections —
  payment, free work, discounts — must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously.
- If results shown are not typical, say so.

## 2. Review gating

**Review gating** means screening customers for sentiment before asking for a
public review — surveying first and inviting only the happy ones.

It violates Google's policies, and it sits close to the FTC's concerns about
distorting the overall body of reviews. Treat it as prohibited.

| Prohibited | Acceptable |
|---|---|
| Survey first, invite only 4–5 star respondents | Ask every completed customer, the same way |
| "If you were happy, review us here; if not, call us" | "We'd appreciate a review" + a separate, always-available service line |
| Unhappy customers routed to a private form instead of the public listing | Feedback form offered to everyone, alongside the review request |
| Asking a customer to remove a negative review as a condition of a fix | Fixing the problem, and letting the customer decide |

The distinction: soliciting reviews broadly and neutrally is fine. Filtering
*who gets asked* by expected sentiment is not.

## 3. Incentives

**Google Business Profile** prohibits offering incentives in exchange for
reviews — discounts, gift cards, entry into a draw, free service.

**Yelp** goes further and discourages soliciting reviews at all, including
neutral requests. Yelp also publicly flags businesses found to be soliciting.

**FTC** treats an undisclosed material connection as deceptive, and an incentive
conditioned on a positive review as prohibited outright.

The safe position: **ask, never pay.** No discount, no draw, no gift, no credit —
not even for "an honest review", because the incentive still distorts who
responds.

## 4. Responding to reviews

Responses are public, permanent, and read by future customers more than by the
reviewer. Assume every response is read by someone deciding whether to call.

**Never**

- Dispute the reviewer's facts publicly, even when they are wrong
- Disclose anything the reviewer did not already make public — the job, the
  address, the amount, their full name, whether they paid
- Imply the reviewer is lying, confused, or a competitor
- Respond while angry, or on the same day, for anything serious
- Offer a remedy the client has not authorised
- Use identical boilerplate across every review — it reads as automated and it
  is visible when the listing is read top to bottom

**Do**

- Acknowledge the experience without conceding legal fault
- Take specifics offline: "we'd like to get this right — please call us at..."
- Keep it short; long responses read as defensive
- Vary the language between responses
- Recommend a phone call when the situation is complex, disputed, or emotional

**Suspected fake reviews.** Report through the platform's flagging process.
Never respond publicly with an accusation, and never threaten. An unfounded
legal threat to remove a review is itself the FTC concern in §1.

**Privacy.** A reviewer disclosing their own details does not license the
business to add more. If the review is about a service call, the response must
not confirm details of that call beyond what the reviewer said.

## 5. Response patterns

**Positive.** Thank them, name something specific from their review, keep it
brief. Do not upsell.

**Fixable complaint.** Acknowledge, state what is being done, move to a private
channel. No excuses, no explanation of company process.

**Factual dispute.** Shortest response of all. Acknowledge that their experience
did not meet expectations, invite a direct conversation, stop. Do not rebut.
**Escalate to a phone call.**

**Suspected fake.** No public response. Flag it. Document why.

**Serious allegation** — safety, discrimination, property damage, injury, legal
threat. **Do not draft a public response at all.** Escalate to the client and to
counsel. A public response here can create real liability.

---

## 6. Checklist

- [ ] No review drafted, templated, or suggested for a customer to post
- [ ] No incentive offered or implied
- [ ] No sentiment-based filtering of who is asked
- [ ] No dispute of the reviewer's facts in public
- [ ] No customer detail beyond what the reviewer made public
- [ ] No accusation, threat, or implication of dishonesty
- [ ] Remedy offered is within what the client authorised
- [ ] Language differs from other recent responses
- [ ] Escalation recommended where the situation warrants it
- [ ] Serious allegations routed to the client and counsel, not answered publicly
- [ ] Marked DRAFT; nothing posted by the agent
