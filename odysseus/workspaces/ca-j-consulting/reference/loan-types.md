# Loan types — CA-J Consulting

**Status:** DRAFT. Not yet approved. `approved: false` in the knowledge manifest.

Structure, typical use, and general trade-offs by loan type. Written for
educational content: **no rates, no payment figures, no qualification
thresholds, and nothing that implies any particular borrower would qualify.**

Where a threshold is genuinely part of a program's definition, it is described
in words rather than numbers, because program details change and a stale figure
in a reference document propagates into everything written from it.

Every entry ends with *questions to ask a lender*, which is the most useful
thing educational content can hand a reader.

# Part 1 — Business lending

## SBA 7(a)

**What it is.** The SBA's primary general-purpose program. A participating
lender makes the loan; the SBA guarantees a portion of it, which reduces the
lender's risk and widens the range of businesses that can be served.

**Typical uses.** Working capital, equipment, refinancing certain business debt,
acquiring a business, and some real estate.

**Structure.** Term loan. Longer terms than most conventional business lending,
with the maximum term varying by use of proceeds — real estate is longest,
working capital shortest. Generally requires a personal guarantee from
significant owners. Fees are set by program rules.

**Trade-offs.** Access and term length are the appeal. The cost is process:
documentation is heavier and timelines are longer than conventional or online
lending. Eligibility rules are program rules, not lender preference, and they
change.

**Ask a lender.** What is the maximum term for my specific use of proceeds? What
fees apply and when? What is your realistic timeline? Is a personal guarantee
required, and from whom? What collateral is expected?

---

## SBA 504

**What it is.** A program for fixed assets, structured across a participating
lender, a Certified Development Company, and the borrower.

**Typical uses.** Owner-occupied commercial real estate and major long-life
equipment. Not working capital.

**Structure.** Split financing across the three parties, with a long fixed-rate
component on the CDC portion. Owner-occupancy requirements apply to real estate.

**Trade-offs.** Long fixed-rate financing on fixed assets is the appeal.
Narrower in purpose than 7(a), with a more complex structure and more parties.

**Ask a lender.** Does my intended use qualify? What are the occupancy
requirements? How do the three pieces fit together, and what does each cost?

---

## SBA Express

**What it is.** A streamlined SBA option with a faster lender decision and a
lower SBA guaranty percentage than standard 7(a).

**Typical uses.** Smaller working capital needs and lines of credit.

**Trade-offs.** Speed, at a lower maximum amount and a smaller guaranty. Because
the lender carries more risk, lender criteria may be tighter.

**Ask a lender.** What is the current maximum under this program? How does your
decision timeline actually compare to standard 7(a)?

---

## Conventional term loan

**What it is.** A lump sum from a bank or lender repaid over a fixed term.

**Typical uses.** Defined, one-time needs — an expansion, a large purchase, a
specific project.

**Structure.** Fixed or variable rate, amortizing, often secured, commonly with
a personal guarantee for a small business.

**Trade-offs.** Straightforward and generally cheaper than alternative finance,
where the business qualifies. Bank criteria are typically the tightest —
established time in business, demonstrated cash flow, and often collateral.

**Ask a lender.** Fixed or variable? Is there a prepayment penalty? What
covenants apply, and what happens if one is breached? What collateral and
guarantees are required?

---

## Business line of credit

**What it is.** Revolving credit up to a limit. Draw, repay, draw again.

**Typical uses.** Managing cash-flow timing — payroll across a slow month,
inventory ahead of a season, the gap between invoicing and payment.

**Structure.** Interest generally accrues only on the drawn balance. Secured or
unsecured. Subject to periodic review, and may include a requirement to rest the
balance at zero for a period.

**Trade-offs.** The right tool for timing problems, and the wrong tool for a
long-lived asset. A line reviewed annually can be reduced or withdrawn, which is
a real risk if the business has come to rely on it.

**Ask a lender.** Is there an annual review, and what could reduce or withdraw
the line? Are there draw or maintenance fees? Is a zero-balance period required?

---

## Equipment financing

**What it is.** Financing secured by the equipment being acquired.

**Typical uses.** Vehicles, machinery, technology — anything with identifiable
resale value.

**Structure.** Term generally matched to the useful life of the asset. The
equipment itself is usually the primary collateral, which can mean less reliance
on other collateral.

**Trade-offs.** Often accessible to businesses that would struggle with
unsecured lending, because the collateral is concrete. The financing is tied to
the asset, and the asset can be repossessed.

**Ask a lender.** Loan or lease, and what happens at the end of the term? Is a
down payment required? Is a personal guarantee required on top of the equipment
collateral? What if the equipment is sold?

---

## Invoice factoring and AR financing

**What it is.** Two related but distinct arrangements. **Factoring** sells
outstanding invoices to a third party at a discount. **AR financing** borrows
against them while the business retains ownership.

**Typical uses.** Businesses invoicing creditworthy customers on payment terms
and needing the cash sooner — common in staffing, freight, and B2B services.

**Structure.** Advance of a portion of invoice value, with the remainder less
fees on collection. **Recourse** leaves the business liable if the customer does
not pay; **non-recourse** shifts some of that risk, defined by the contract.

**Trade-offs.** Availability depends heavily on the customers' credit rather than
the business's own, which can suit a young company with strong customers. In
factoring, the factor may contact customers directly — a real consideration for
the relationship. Cost is generally higher than conventional lending.

**Ask a lender.** Recourse or non-recourse, and precisely what does non-recourse
cover? Who contacts my customers? What is the full fee structure including
anything charged on aging invoices? Is there a minimum volume commitment?

---

## Merchant cash advance

**What it is.** A purchase of a portion of future receivables. Structured as a
purchase rather than a loan, which may place it outside some lending
regulations.

**Typical uses.** Fast access to capital, typically for businesses with card
volume, often where other options are unavailable.

**Structure.** A lump sum in exchange for an agreed repayment amount, collected
as a fixed daily or weekly remittance or a percentage of card receipts. Priced
with a **factor rate**, not an interest rate.

**Trade-offs — state these plainly.** Speed and accessibility are the appeal.
Cost is generally the highest in business financing, and the structure is
frequently misunderstood:

- A factor rate is not an interest rate and not an APR. The effective annualized
  cost is often far above what the multiplier suggests.
- Early repayment typically does **not** reduce the total owed, unless the
  contract says otherwise.
- Daily or weekly remittance can strain cash flow in a way monthly payments do
  not.
- Stacking multiple advances is a recognized route into serious distress.

Educational content should present the mechanics honestly and let the reader
draw the conclusion. Explaining the cost structure clearly is education.
Telling a reader not to take one is advice.

**Ask a funder.** What is the total repayment amount, in dollars? What is the
remittance amount and frequency? Does early repayment reduce the total? Is a
personal guarantee or confession of judgment required? What happens if receipts
fall?

---

## Commercial real estate loan

**What it is.** Financing secured by commercial property.

**Typical uses.** Purchasing, refinancing, or improving owner-occupied or
investment property.

**Structure.** Frequently a term shorter than the amortization period, producing
a **balloon** at maturity. Underwriting commonly leans on DSCR and LTV.
Owner-occupied and investment property are underwritten differently.

**Trade-offs.** Long-term control of premises and the possibility of building
equity. The balloon structure means refinancing risk at maturity, in whatever
market exists then — the most under-appreciated feature of these loans.

**Ask a lender.** What is the term versus the amortization, and is there a
balloon? What DSCR is required? Is there a prepayment penalty, and how is it
calculated? What happens at maturity if conditions have changed?

# Part 2 — Mortgage lending

> Every entry below is process education only. No rate, payment, down payment,
> qualification threshold, or eligibility figure appears here or in any content
> written from it. See `compliance-guardrails.md` §4.

## Conventional conforming

**What it is.** A mortgage meeting the criteria to be purchased by the
government-sponsored enterprises, including a maximum loan amount set annually
and varying by county.

**Structure.** Fixed or adjustable. Mortgage insurance is generally required
where borrower equity is below a threshold, and the rules for when it ends
differ from government programs.

**Ask a lender.** What is the conforming limit in this county this year? When
does mortgage insurance end, and what is required to remove it?

---

## Jumbo

**What it is.** A mortgage above the conforming limit, not eligible for GSE
purchase.

**Trade-offs.** Enables higher-value purchases. Because the loan is not sold
into the GSE channel, underwriting standards are set by the lender or investor
and are typically more demanding on reserves, documentation, and credit.

**Ask a lender.** What reserve and documentation requirements apply? Is the loan
held in portfolio or sold?

---

## FHA

**What it is.** A mortgage insured by the Federal Housing Administration, made
by an approved lender.

**Structure.** More flexible credit and equity requirements than conventional,
in exchange for mortgage insurance premiums. Property condition standards apply,
which can affect which homes are eligible.

**Ask a lender.** How long do the insurance premiums last on my structure — and
does that differ from conventional mortgage insurance? What property conditions
could affect eligibility?

---

## VA

**What it is.** A mortgage guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs for
eligible service members, veterans, and certain surviving spouses.

**Structure.** No mortgage insurance requirement, with a funding fee that varies
and is waived in some circumstances. Eligibility is established by a Certificate
of Eligibility. Appraisal and property requirements apply.

**Ask a lender.** How do I obtain a Certificate of Eligibility? Does the funding
fee apply in my circumstances? Has my entitlement been used before?

---

## USDA

**What it is.** A program for eligible rural and some suburban properties, with
household income limits.

**Structure.** Property must be in an eligible area — a map-based determination —
and household income must fall within program limits for that area.

**Ask a lender.** Is this specific address in an eligible area? What are the
current income limits for this county and household size?

---

## Fixed-rate vs adjustable-rate

**Fixed.** The rate does not change. Predictable for the full term.

**Adjustable.** An initial fixed period, then periodic adjustment to an index
plus a margin, subject to caps.

**The trade-off, stated neutrally.** A fixed rate transfers rate risk to the
lender; an adjustable rate keeps it with the borrower in exchange for different
initial pricing. Which is appropriate depends on circumstances this workspace
does not evaluate.

**Ask a lender.** Which index and what margin? What are the caps — per
adjustment, and over the life? How often does it adjust after the initial
period? What would my payment be at the maximum cap?

---

## Refinancing

**Rate-and-term.** Replaces the existing mortgage with new terms.

**Cash-out.** Replaces it with a larger loan, taking the difference in cash. The
borrower is converting equity into debt secured by the home.

**Trade-offs.** Refinancing carries closing costs, and resets the amortization
schedule — a factor often overlooked when comparing payments alone. Cash-out
increases the balance secured by the property.

**Ask a lender.** What are the total closing costs? How does the new
amortization compare to what remains on the current loan? Is there a prepayment
penalty on the existing loan?

---

## HELOC and home equity loan

**HELOC.** Revolving credit secured by home equity, typically a draw period then
a repayment period. Rate is commonly variable.

**Home equity loan.** A lump sum secured by equity, typically fixed-rate and
amortizing — a second mortgage.

**Trade-offs.** Flexibility for the HELOC, predictability for the fixed loan.
Both are secured by the home, which is the point to make clearly in any
educational content: the consequence of non-payment is not the same as with
unsecured credit. HELOC payments commonly rise substantially when the draw
period ends.

**Ask a lender.** When does the draw period end and how does the payment change?
Is the rate variable, and what are the caps? Are there annual or inactivity fees?

---

## DSCR and investor loans

**What it is.** Financing for investment property underwritten primarily on the
property's income rather than the borrower's personal income.

**Structure.** Qualification leans on DSCR. Terms generally differ from
owner-occupied lending.

**Ask a lender.** What DSCR is required? How is rental income calculated —
market rent, actual leases, or a discount to either? What reserves are required?

---

## Non-QM

**What it is.** Mortgages that do not meet the Qualified Mortgage standard —
including bank-statement programs for self-employed borrowers, asset-depletion
programs, and others.

**Trade-offs.** Serves borrowers whose income is real but does not document in
the conventional way. Terms and pricing differ from QM lending, and the
protections associated with QM status do not apply.

**Ask a lender.** How is my income being documented and calculated? What
specifically makes this loan non-QM? What features does it carry that a QM loan
would not?
